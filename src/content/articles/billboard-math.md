A client asked me this week whether he should start a second brand. Same buildings, new name, aimed at a new part of the country. He also asked whether his steel buildings and his wood buildings should each get their own website. He has asked me a version of this three times this year. Every time I say no. Every time I explain it badly. This is the explanation I owed him.

## Billboard math

Picture a highway. You have a billboard. Your competitor has a billboard. It feels fair to say you each have a shot at half the drivers.

Now you put up two more. Three signs for you, one for them. You expect to take three of every four drivers who look up. That is how most of us picture visibility. More signs, more share. I call it billboard math.

Billboard math is right on a highway. Every sign gets seen. It is wrong in search, and it is wrong in a way that costs real money.

## Search is a line, not a wall

When 100 people search for the thing you sell, Google does not show them a wall of signs. It shows them a list. The first result takes the biggest share of those 100 people. Every result below it takes a steeply smaller share.

Here is roughly how it falls, using [Ubersuggest's](https://neilpatel.com/ubersuggest/) click model for a normal ten-result page. First place takes about 30 of the 100. Second takes about 16. Third takes about 10. Fifth takes about 5. Eighth takes about 2. Tenth takes about 1. Page two gets almost nothing. [Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/seo-statistics/) puts 96.6% of all Google clicks on page one. When an AI answer sits above the results, [the top result loses about 58% of its clicks](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-overviews-reduce-clicks-update/) on top of that.

So there is one spot that pays well. The front of the line. Everything behind it gets crumbs.

Now the part that matters. Your place in that line is earned by your website, not by your company. Every link pointing at you, every person who types your name, every page you have published for years attaches to one domain. A new domain starts at zero. It does not matter that you own both.

## What a second site actually does

A second brand selling the same product wants the same spot in the same line. Google gives that spot to one page. Not both.

The new site starts on page two. The old site gets a little weaker, because the links and mentions and new pages that used to pile up in one place now land in two.

Say your one site holds third place. That is about 10 of every 100 searchers. Split the same effort across three sites and none of them holds third. Say they land fifth, eighth and tenth. That is 5 plus 2 plus 1. Eight of 100, and that is the generous version. You did not triple anything. You gave up the only place in line that pays.

Search people call this cannibalization. You are not adding a competitor to the market. You are adding yourself as your own competitor, in a line that only pays the front. Google's spam policy even has a name for the extreme version, [doorway pages](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies#doorway-abuse), which are several sites or pages built to catch the same searches and funnel people to one place.

Brand searches make it worse. People who type your name are already sold. A new name has none of those people on day one. A new name that borrows half of your old name splits the ones you already had, because Google now has two candidates for the same brand.

## When a second brand is right

A second brand is right when it sells a different product to a different buyer. Those buyers are standing in a different line. The two sites can link to each other, share a little authority, and never fight over a search.

The brand research lands in the same place from the other direction. [Hsu, Fournier and Srinivasan](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-014-0422-5) found in 2016 that sub-brands do not wall off risk from the parent company. They spread it. [Rao, Agarwal and Dahlhoff](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.68.4.126.42735) found in 2004 that companies mixing a corporate brand with sub-brands are valued lower than companies that commit to one house brand. Commit to one name, or build a brand with no visible tie to the first. The middle is the worst place to stand.

## One house, many rooms

A new region is a location, not a product. It belongs on the site you already have. A page for the region. A Google Business Profile for the branch. A dropdown that shows the buyer the facts for their state.

A new material is the same. Steel and wood are two rooms in one house, not two houses.

Three billboards get seen three times. Three websites stand in one line, and the line only pays the front.
